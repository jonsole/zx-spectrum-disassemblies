Room structure

Pentagram's world is 139 rooms of forest and ruin, each a record in the room directory at ROOMS. This page draws every one of them with the game's own code, puts them together into one map in the game's own projection, marks on it where the game's things are, and gives each room's record decoded. Knight Lore, whose engine this is, keeps its castle on a numbered grid (its rooms); Pentagram does not, and working out its map is part of what follows.

What is measured and what is read. The pictures are measured: each room is built and drawn by the game in a simulator, and read out of its screen buffer. So are the things found in the rooms as built (the well, lifts, crumbling blocks), the deadly things (from the bits the game sets, after a turn), and the doorways (each one walked through). Read from the game's tables: the record format, the doorways' destinations and walls, the start rooms, the quest records and the collectables' places. Worked out from those, and not something the game itself holds anywhere: the layout of the map.

The world

No arithmetic on room numbers leads from one room to the next. A doorway is a scenery entry whose template stands the two pillars of an arch in one of the walls, and its second byte is the number of the room it leads to: the room builder (BUILD_ROOM) copies that byte into every piece's +$08, and the first pillar's update routine (FIRST_PILLAR) sends him there once he is through. Which wall the arch is in, the template says by where it puts the pillars: at the high or the low U wall, or the high or the low V wall. On the screen, +U runs down to the right and +V up to the right (CALC_PIXEL_XY); here +U is called east and +V north. Leaving by the east wall he arrives at the west wall of the next room, lined up with its arch (ADJUST_PLYR_UVZ_FOR_ROOM_SIZE).

There are 290 doorways, and every one has a partner: the room it leads to has a doorway in the opposite wall leading back (290 of 290). Taking each doorway as one step the way it faces, and starting anywhere, every room gets a square, and every loop comes back to where it started: no two routes to a room disagree. The squares span 19 columns by 18 rows. But the world is not quite flat: rooms 139-144 land on the squares of 39-41, 55-57; rooms 145-146 land on the squares of 87-88; room 148 lands on the squares of 3. A doorway only names a room, so nothing in the game needs the rooms to fit on a plan, and nothing stops two of them sharing a place: walking through one, the player cannot see the other. On the map each of these is drawn aside, on a darker floor, and a line joins each of its doorways to the room it leads to.

Below, each room is drawn by the game and put on its square, in the game's projection, on its floor; the back rooms are pasted first, so nearer rooms stand in front. Click a room for its entry, or see the whole world at full size. The markers show where things are, each from the game's own tables or from the rooms as the game builds them; tick a box to show its layer.

The doorways

270 doorways are at floor level and 20 are raised: their arch stands on a ledge (the scenery templates with graphic 11 beside them), and each raised doorway's partner on the other side is at floor level (20 of 20). Every doorway was tried in the simulator when this page was built: the room built, the player stood 12 units inside the wall in the middle of the arch (or, where something stands there, at the nearest clear spot further in), facing out, and the walk key held, with any death taken back at each turn so that what is tested is whether the way is open. 268 of the 270 floor-level doorways let him straight through; 2 more only with jump held as well (room 38's north doorway, room 78's west doorway); none is blocked. Standing on the ledge of a raised doorway and walking out took him through 20 of the 20. In all 290, he came out in the room the doorway's byte names. So no doorway is shut and none leads one way only, except that a raised one must be climbed to.

Room 147's south doorway leads to room 0: a destination byte of zero is a real doorway, like any other, since the pillar's routine takes whatever the byte holds.

A room record

The records are packed end to end at ROOMS, with no index: BUILD_ROOM finds a room by stepping from one record to the next until the number matches. In order:

ByteWhat
0The room's number.
1How many bytes follow: the distance from this byte to the next record. The builder counts them down and stops wherever they run out -- even partway through a group of objects (rooms 13, 108 have a group cut short this way).
2Bits 0-2 the ink the whole room is drawn in (the builder adds BRIGHT); bits 3-7 its size, an index into ROOM_SIZES.
3 ...The scenery, two bytes an entry: a scenery template, an index into SCENERY_TABLE (see the scenery), and a byte the builder copies into +$08 of every piece: for a doorway, the room it leads to; for other scenery, zero.
$FFThe end of the scenery, if objects follow.
groupsA byte whose bits 3-7 are an object template, an index into OBJECT_TABLE (see the templates), and bits 0-2 the number of copies less one; then a position byte for each copy. Template 31 would switch to a second page of templates; no room uses it.

A position byte is a cell of the floor and a level: bits 0-2 the U cell and bits 3-5 the V cell of an 8 by 8 grid, bits 6-7 the level. The builder makes U = 72 + 16 times the U cell, V likewise, and Z = the floor's height + 12 times the level. Scenery carries its own coordinates, so a scenery template always stands in the same place in a room.
